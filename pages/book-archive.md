| May 2024       | _The Shallows_                                           | Nicholas Carr              | In the end, I think this book is mis-titled. It's really a cautionary history of technology as it relates to our humanity. The internet is just the major case study.<br><br>At first, it was slow-going. I started reading, ready to doubt the seriousness of any of the problems that were to be presented. And Carr didn't get everything right. For example, his concerns about ebooks turning into a fundamentally different medium than print books didn't come to pass.<br><br>However, it gradually became clear to me that Carr and many thinkers he references (such as Marshall McLuhan and Joseph Weizenbaum) are on to something. I could see the habit-forming pull of technology in my own life, and I found the criticisms of reductive metaphors (such as that our minds are like computers) and of psychological misconceptions (such as thinking we can run out of long-term memory) very convincing.<br><br>In the end, this is a very humanistic book. It acknowledges the wonder of technology, but it makes an argument for the uniqueness and the value of the human mind. The slowness of thought that is needed to empathize and learn deeply. The livingness of a human brain. In doing so, it seems to warn against technological fatalism (though Carr doesn't state it directly).<br><br>The defense of humanness and the rejection of the mind-as-a-machine seem all the more relevant in the current climate where we seem to again be on the cusp of adopting a new technology in all areas of society: generative AI. The emotional delusions average people had about Weisenbaum's ELIZA program seem all the more threatening in light of much more powerful thought-simulators.<br><br>I've loved technology all my life. I've made a career out of advancing technology built using internet primitives, no less. But, this book has served to cool my ardor and make me take a closer look at the potential drawbacks of technological advancement.<br><br>In the end, I found myself hoping with the author that "we won't go gently into the future our computer engineers and software programmers are scripting for us." |

| December 2018  | _Growth of the Soil_                                     | Knut Hamsun                | Growth of the Soil strikes me above all as an allegory. It is not a preachy story of flat characters with blatantly obvious names like the ones in The Pilgrim's Progress. All the same, the story is not concerned with individual characters as ends in and of themselves, so much as it is with their causal actions and revealing destinies. It is in this sense that Growth of the Soil is allegorical. It is like an Icelandic saga which narrates a mythology. We have no subjective stream-of-consciousness narrative as we find in _Sult_, and this must certainly be on purpose. The inner workings of the characters' minds, though interesting to speculate about, are not the point. Instead we are meant to learn from them how to live and how not to live. At the center of it all stands Isak, the "lord of creation (12)" who clearly garners Hamsun's approval, as he is blessed with the greatest destiny. Given this, it is only a small jump to accept Isak as an embodiment of Hamsun's ideals. Isak illustrates Hamsun's belief that man and nature are at their best when they find themselves in a symbiotic relationship.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
